Should you pay points to lower the rate?

Discount points are an upfront cost in exchange for a lower interest rate. This draft explains the idea and the break-even comparison, without quoting current rates.

What are mortgage points?

A discount point is a fee paid at closing, usually equal to a percentage of the loan amount, in exchange for a lower interest rate. The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau describes discount points as a form of prepaid interest.

This page does not quote today’s rates, point prices, or monthly payments. Those figures change and have to come from a current Loan Factory scenario, not from an article.

How a break-even comparison works

A common way to think about points is a break-even period: divide the upfront cost of the points by the reduction in the monthly principal-and-interest payment. The result is a rough number of months before the lower payment has offset the upfront cost, if you keep the loan that long.

That comparison is incomplete if you ignore other closing costs, tax treatment, how long you expect to keep the mortgage, and whether you might refinance again. It is a worksheet, not a recommendation.

Permanent buydowns and temporary buydowns

A permanent buydown lowers the note rate for the life of the loan, in exchange for points paid at closing. A temporary buydown lowers the payment for an opening period (for example, the first year or two) and then the payment steps up. Seller-paid and lender-paid buydowns also exist; who pays, and how the cost is reflected in the price or the rate, is a loan-level fact.

When points may or may not help

Points tend to be more useful when you expect to keep the loan past the break-even period and less useful if you may sell or refinance sooner. Paying points is optional in many cases. Whether the tradeoff is reasonable depends on the actual cost, the actual rate reduction, and your timeline. This is not a claim that buying points will save money.
